# Objection Handling Playbook

## Mindset First
Objections are not rejections — they're unresolved questions dressed up as pushback. Your job is to understand the real concern underneath.

**Listen → Acknowledge → Reframe → Solve**

---

## The Core Objections

### "It's too expensive."

**What they really mean**: I don't see enough value to justify this yet.

**Response approach:**
1. Don't defend the price immediately
2. Ask: "Too expensive compared to what?"
3. Reframe to cost of the problem: "What's the cost if you don't solve this in Q3?"
4. If budget is genuinely limited: "What would a starting scope look like that fits your budget?"

**Go-to line:**
"The investment is [X]. The cost of a 3-month delay in hiring is usually [Y]. Our clients typically see payback in [Z]. Does that math change the picture?"

---

### "We hire directly / we have an internal TA team."

**What they really mean**: I'm not sure you're better than what I already have.

**Response:**
"That makes sense — most of our clients have TA teams. The question we ask is: what roles is your team consistently struggling to close in < 60 days? That's usually where we add the most value — not replacing your team, complementing them on the hard stuff."

---

### "We need to think about it / not the right time."

**What they really mean**: Not urgent enough yet, OR I don't have internal buy-in.

**Response:**
"Totally fair. Can I ask — is it more that the timing isn't right, or is there something specific that needs to be solved internally first? That'll help me know how to stay helpful without being a pest."

Then: agree on a specific re-engage date. "Let's plan to reconnect on [date] — does that work?"

---

### "We tried an outside firm before and it didn't work."

**What they really mean**: I have a bad experience and I'm protecting myself.

**Response:**
"Tell me more about what happened — I want to understand what went wrong." [Listen fully]
Then: "What you're describing — [reflect their experience] — is exactly the gap our model is designed to close. Here's specifically how it's different: [2-3 concrete differences]."

---

### "Can you send me some info?"

**What they really mean**: I'm not sold on spending time with you yet.

**Response:**
"Happy to. So I make it useful rather than generic — what specifically would help you most? Case studies? How the model works? Pricing overview?"

Then send exactly what they asked for + a follow-up time.

---

### "We're not sure we have budget for this."

**What they really mean**: I haven't fought for budget internally, OR I'm testing your flexibility.

**Response:**
"Understood. Is this a case where budget exists but needs approval, or is it more that the need hasn't been prioritized yet?"
- If budget exists: help them build the internal business case
- If not prioritized: dig into urgency — what would need to be true for this to get prioritized?

## Teaching Side Quest Objections

### "Is this relevant for someone who's not technical?"
"The entire point of the AI for Sales track is that you don't need to be technical. Everything I teach is built for people who sell — not people who code."

### "I can just learn this from YouTube for free."
"You can. And I'd recommend starting there. If you're looking for a structured path with real sales use cases and a community to work through it with, that's what the course offers."

### "I'm not sure I need to learn sales / I just need product-market fit."
"Sales IS product-market fit discovery. Every founder conversation is a discovery call. Learning this earlier would have saved me 18 months."
